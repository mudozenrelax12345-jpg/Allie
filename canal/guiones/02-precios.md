# Vídeo 2 · El precio ya no es del producto

**Tesis:** todo el mundo está enfadado porque «usan tus datos para cobrarte más».
Delta le ha dicho al Congreso por escrito que no usa datos personales, y es
probable que sea verdad. El problema es que las leyes que están escribiendo
veinticuatro estados prohíben exactamente esa palabra — datos personales — y el
sistema no la necesita.

**Enlaza con el vídeo 1:** allí la IA abarató *vigilar*, y vigilar se expandió.
Aquí abarató *adivinar lo que estás dispuesto a pagar*. Y el senador Blumenthal
firmó las dos cartas.

**Duración estimada:** 10 minutos · ~1.530 palabras a 152 palabras por minuto.

**Arranque:** la cifra va en el segundo cero. El vídeo 1 tardaba nueve segundos
en dar el número que prometía la miniatura, y la gente se iba en el minuto y
medio. Aquí el dato está en pantalla antes de que termine la primera frase.

---

### Acto 1 · El gancho

> Three quarters of the items in one grocery basket came back at a different price depending on who was looking. Up to twenty-three percent apart. Same shop, same products, same moment.

> That is not a glitch. It is the business model. And this year, three American states made it illegal.

> The laws are not going to work. I want to show you exactly why, and the reason is one answer somebody gave the Senate in August.

> The man answering was Robert Hedges. He used to be the chief data officer at Visa. He was asked what these pricing systems actually look at.

> He described a system that asks three questions. When did you book the ticket. Have you already booked the hotel. Do you have friends or relatives in the area.

> Stop on that. Not your name. Not your income. Not your browsing history.

> None of those three things is your personal data. They are facts about your situation.

> Five weeks later, Delta Air Lines wrote to Congress. There is no fare product, the letter says, that targets customers with individualized prices based on personal data.

> I think that sentence may be completely true. This video is about why that does not help you.

---

### Acto 2 · Lo que la máquina mira de verdad

> First, what we are actually talking about. The phrase that has taken hold is surveillance pricing. The idea is that a company watches you, works out how much you will pay, and charges you that.

> This is not a theory. In twenty twenty-four, the Federal Trade Commission ran a study and identified around two hundred and fifty companies using pricing vendors of this kind.

> That grocery basket I opened with came from a test the Groundwork Collaborative presented at the same hearing. They filled it on Instacart from different accounts. More than three quarters of the items came back at different prices, with gaps of up to twenty-three percent for identical products.

> Consumer Reports ran a similar test on ride-hailing. Users requesting the exact same trip, within seconds of each other, were shown different base rates.

> Investigators in Minnesota found that prices in Target's app changed when the customer physically entered the store.

> And here is the one that should settle any argument about whether this is new. In twenty twenty-three, drivers with perfect records but poor credit scores were paying up to a hundred and fifteen percent more for car insurance than identical drivers with good credit.

> That is not artificial intelligence. That is a spreadsheet. Charging different people different prices for the same thing is one of the oldest ideas in commerce.

> What changed is not the idea. What changed is how cheap it became to do it to everyone, individually, in real time.

> But look again at the examples. The Instacart basket. The Uber fare. The Target app.

> Notice what is actually doing the work in each one. It is not your name.

> It is when you shopped. Where you were standing. Which device you were holding. Whether you came back to look again.

> The machine does not need to know who you are. It needs to know what kind of buyer you are behaving like right now.

> And that distinction, which sounds like a technicality, turns out to be the whole story.

---

### Acto 3 · La negación

> Let us go back to Delta, because Delta is where this got loud.

> In July of twenty twenty-five, Delta confirmed it was working with an artificial intelligence company called Fetcherr to help set fares. At the time, the system was influencing prices on around three percent of tickets, and Delta said it wanted to reach twenty percent by the end of the year.

> The chief executive, Ed Bastian, had said that artificial intelligence could eventually improve the airline's profitability by as much as fifty percent.

> The reaction was immediate. On the twenty-first of July, three senators wrote to Bastian. Ruben Gallego, Mark Warner, and Richard Blumenthal.

> If that third name sounds familiar, it should. Blumenthal is the same senator who wrote to Hertz about the damage scanners. Same senator, same concern, a year apart.

> Their letter contains a sentence I keep coming back to. Prices could be dictated not by supply and demand, but by individual need.

> In March of this year, the House Oversight Committee sent a similar letter to Expedia, asking how consumer data is used alongside its pricing algorithms.

> And then, on the ninth of September, Delta replied to Congressman Frank Pallone.

> The letter is a flat denial. Delta does not provide the pricing tool with personal information about its customers. The fares it files do not take any customer's personal data into account. The tool, it says, uses route-level demand and market data to support human analysts.

> Now. Read those two things side by side.

> Delta is using artificial intelligence to set prices. Delta says that artificial intelligence does not see your personal data.

> Both of those can be true at the same time. They are not the same claim, and the second one is not a denial of the first.

---

### Acto 4 · Por qué «datos personales» es la bisagra equivocada

> This is the part I want to be careful with, because it would be easy to overreach here and I am not going to.

> I have no evidence that Delta is doing anything other than what it says in that letter. Nobody has produced any. I am not alleging that it is.

> What I am saying is narrower, and I think more useful. The thing everyone is arguing about is the wrong thing.

> Go back to what the former Visa data officer told the Senate. When did you book. Have you booked the hotel. Do you have people in the area.

> Ask yourself which of those a law about personal data would stop.

> A booking time is not personal data. A route is not personal data. The gap between when you searched and when you bought is not personal data. The fact that a flight into a particular airport in a particular week correlates with a funeral, or a graduation, or a hospital visit, is not personal data either.

> It is a pattern. And a pattern is enough.

> A system does not need to know that you are you. It needs to know that people who behave the way you are behaving right now will pay more, and that you are probably one of them.

> That is not surveillance in the sense people picture. Nobody is reading your messages. It is inference, and it is cheap, and it is legal almost everywhere.

> Which brings us to the laws.

---

### Acto 5 · Lo que las leyes prohíben en realidad

> Twenty twenty-six was the year this reached statute books. Across the country, twenty-four states introduced more than forty bills on algorithmic and surveillance pricing.

> Three of them signed laws.

> Maryland's took effect on the first of October. New Jersey's was signed in July. Connecticut's in June.

> The governor of New Jersey, Mikie Sherrill, put the reasoning plainly. The last thing people need, she said, is companies secretly using their personal data to charge them more than someone else for the exact same product.

> I agree with her. And now look at what the laws actually cover.

> Maryland's applies to grocery stores over fifteen thousand square feet, and to food delivery. New Jersey's applies to retailers setting grocery prices, and it pauses new electronic shelf labels for a year while the state studies them. Connecticut's covers retailers and third-party delivery, with an exception for price differences unrelated to personal data.

> So: groceries. Delivery apps. Shelf labels.

> The clearest, loudest, most documented example in the entire debate is an airline ticket. And an airline ticket is not covered by any of them.

> And every one of those laws hinges on the same two words. Personal data. The input the machine has just told us, under oath, that it does not need.

---

### Acto 6 · Qué significa

> So here is where I land, and as always you are welcome to land somewhere else.

> A price used to be a fact about a thing. It was printed on the thing. Everyone in the shop saw the same number, and the number was about the product.

> A price is now, increasingly, a guess about you. Not about who you are. About how you are behaving, which is a better predictor anyway.

> In the last video, about Hertz and its damage scanners, the conclusion was that artificial intelligence had made enforcement cheap, and that when you make enforcement cheap, enforcement expands to fill the space.

> This is the same shape, one step further along. Working out what a specific person would pay used to cost more than the extra money it brought in. Now it costs almost nothing.

> And when inference gets cheap, pricing stops being a decision about a product and becomes a decision about a customer.

> I do not think the laws being written are wrong to exist. I think they were written against the headline rather than the mechanism. They ban the word everyone is angry about, and the mechanism does not require that word.

> A rule aimed at personal data will stop a company from pricing you by name. It will not stop a company from pricing you by pattern. And pattern is what works.

> I cannot tell you that any particular airline is doing this.

> I can tell you that the rule being written this year would not catch it if one were.
