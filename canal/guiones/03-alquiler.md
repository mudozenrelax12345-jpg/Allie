# Vídeo 3 · Las casas vacías a propósito

**Tesis:** el programa que fija los alquileres en millones de pisos americanos
llegó a recomendar dejar viviendas vacías, porque subir el precio de las
ocupadas compensaba de sobra. La defensa siempre fue la misma: es solo una
recomendación. El 29 de septiembre de 2026 un juez federal le dio a esa frase
rango constitucional — una recomendación de precio es expresión comercial,
protegida por la Primera Enmienda.

**Enlaza con los dos anteriores:** en el vídeo 1 la IA abarató *vigilar*. En el
2, *adivinar lo que estás dispuesto a pagar*. Aquí abarata *coordinarse sin
hablar con nadie*.

**Duración:** 6 minutos · ~915 palabras a 152 palabras por minuto.

**Por qué seis y no diez.** El vídeo 1 duraba 9:42 y la gente se iba en el
1:21. Hacer once minutos para un espectador que se va en el minuto y medio es
construir para alguien que todavía no existe. Mismo contenido, la mitad de
aire.

**Lo que se ve.** Esta vez la pantalla no repite la frase que se acaba de oír.
Demuestra: una rejilla de cuarenta mil ventanas que se apagan, una barra de
ocupación que baja, diez recomendaciones de las que nueve se aceptan, una
línea de tiempo que se dibuja, y el documento real con la frase subrayándose.
Lo único que se puede leer en pantalla y no se oye son los datos.

---

### Acto 1 · Mil casas vacías

> About a thousand apartments, kept empty on purpose.

> Not abandoned. Not being repaired. Empty because a piece of software worked out that the rent on the other thirty-nine thousand would more than cover them. It was right. Revenue went up three to four percent.

> This is one American landlord, forty thousand units, in 2017. Before the software, its buildings ran about ninety-eight percent full. After it, ninety-five.

> The chief executive of the software company told investors it had been very hard for executives to accept that they could operate at ninety-four or ninety-six percent and make more money. Hard to accept. That is the interesting part.

> A person looks at a thousand empty flats in a housing shortage and feels something. The software does not. One of the men who built it said exactly that: we said there's way too much empathy going on here.

> The company is RealPage. Its customers manage close to twenty million rental units. The Justice Department sued it, eight states joined, and last month a federal judge ruled that its rent recommendations are speech, protected by the First Amendment.

> That is not a loophole somebody found. It is the thing working as designed.

---

### Acto 2 · Es solo una recomendación

> Here is what it does. A landlord hands over every unit, every lease, every renewal date. The software also holds the same from other landlords using it. Not published numbers. Theirs. Then every day it says what to charge.

> RealPage's answer has always been one sentence, and it is a good one. It is only a recommendation. The landlord decides.

> Former employees told ProPublica that as many as ninety percent of those recommendations get taken. One former executive said that a property accepting all of them basically has a blindfold on and is pushing a button.

> The Justice Department's complaint describes an auto-accept setting, and advisers whose job included watching whether customers complied. In Washington DC, the attorney general alleges landlords followed the suggested rent more than ninety percent of the time. That is an allegation. No court has ruled on it.

> But hold the number. Nine times out of ten.

> Because the law does not ban coordination. It bans agreement. Two landlords in a room deciding not to undercut each other is a crime, and has been for a century. Two landlords who never speak, who both do what the same machine said — there is no room, no conversation, nothing to produce in court.

---

### Acto 3 · Lo que pasó después

> August 2024, the Justice Department sues. Fifteen months later it settles. No fine. No damages. No admission of wrongdoing.

> What RealPage agreed to is real: competitors' live data out of the engine, training only on data a year old, no models narrower than a state, a court-appointed monitor. It is a rebuilt product. But nobody paid and nobody admitted anything.

> Money moved somewhere else. The private cases, brought by renters, have been settling one after another. A hundred and forty-two million by October 2025. Two hundred and eighteen million more in May 2026.

> So the government case produced a monitor and no money, and the renters' cases produced nearly four hundred million. To be clear, because it matters: no court has found that RealPage or any of these landlords broke the law. Every one of them settled while denying it.

---

### Acto 4 · El veintinueve de septiembre

> In October 2025 New York banned it. Not the agreement — the tool. It became illegal to operate software that facilitates an agreement between landlords not to compete.

> RealPage sued the state. On the twenty-ninth of September, Judge Valerie Caproni, in Manhattan, paused the ban.

> The argument is the First Amendment. The court found that an algorithmically-informed recommendation of a housing price is commercial speech, and that RealPage's business is the communication of pricing information to its users — which is exactly what the law regulates.

> Read that slowly. The number the software sends is a statement. Statements are speech. Speech is protected.

> New York did not fail because it was weak. It failed because it was precise. It named the tool, and naming the tool meant regulating what the tool says.

---

### Acto 5 · Qué significa

> This is one judge, one state, one stage of one case. It can be reversed, and Arizona, New Jersey and Washington DC are all still litigating.

> But the law has one word at its centre, and the word is agreement. Everything is built on the idea that harm takes two parties deciding together.

> A recommendation taken nine times out of ten is not two parties deciding anything. It is one party publishing, and a thousand others agreeing with the publication. We have a word for a lot of people independently reaching the same conclusion. We call it a market.

> Scanning a car used to cost money. Guessing what you would pay used to need your data. Coordinating used to need a conversation.

> Now it needs a subscription. And a conversation is evidence. A subscription is a receipt.
