# The Eleven Words Shops Went to Court to Avoid

**Tesis:** la discusión pública sobre los precios con algoritmo da por hecho
que el algoritmo sube el precio. El dato que nadie mira dice que la mitad de
las veces lo baja — y ese dato es, literalmente, el argumento jurídico con el
que las tiendas intentan tumbar la ley que les obliga a avisarte.

Narración en inglés. El generador solo lee las líneas que empiezan por `> `
dentro de un apartado `### Acto …`. Todo lo demás se ignora.

### Acto 1 · El gancho

*Imagen: una etiqueta de precio digital en una estantería. Corte a una sala de vistas vacía.*

> New York passed a law that makes shops put eleven words next to a price.
>
> Here they are. This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data.
>
> That is the whole law. Not a ban. Not a limit on what they can charge. A label.
>
> The shops sued.
>
> And the interesting part is what they are not arguing. They are not saying the label is false. They agree it is true.
>
> Their argument, in their own words, is that it is literally true but misleading.
>
> I want to show you why that argument might work, because the reason is a number almost nobody has looked at.

### Acto 2 · Qué dice la ley y quién la pelea

*Imagen: el Capitolio del estado de Nueva York. Tarjeta con el texto del aviso.*

> The law is called the Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act. If a shop uses an algorithm that draws on your personal data to set a price, it has to say so next to the product.
>
> The penalty is not dramatic. The attorney general sends a cease and desist letter. If the business does not fix it, up to one thousand dollars a violation.
>
> The group suing is the National Retail Federation. It represents department stores, grocers, discount chains and restaurant chains. It sued the attorney general of New York, Letitia James, in twenty twenty-five.
>
> Their claim is the First Amendment. The government, they say, is forcing them to speak.
>
> And this is not a small corner of the economy. A Federal Trade Commission staff report in January of twenty twenty-five found that the middlemen selling these pricing tools were working with at least two hundred and fifty clients. A survey of more than two thousand two hundred business leaders across twenty-eight countries found that twenty-eight percent were already using artificial intelligence for real-time price optimisation.
>
> In twenty twenty-six, legislators in at least thirty states looked at bills about pricing.

### Acto 3 · El número que nadie mira

*Imagen: un gráfico de precios subiendo y bajando. Tarjeta grande: 50%.*

> Here is the number.
>
> A web data company called Decodo tracked price changes across American retail through twenty twenty-five. Electronics. Clothing. Home improvement.
>
> About half of the price changes it recorded were decreases.
>
> Half. Down.
>
> That is not what the public argument sounds like. The public argument sounds like a machine that reads your phone and charges you more because it knows you are desperate.
>
> Sometimes it is that. A separate investigation found shoppers on one delivery app seeing different prices for identical products at the same shops. The company later said it had been running randomised tests, and it stopped.
>
> But sometimes the machine cuts the price to move stock, or to beat the shop across the road. And the label does not distinguish between those two things. It says the same eleven words either way.
>
> That is the retailers' whole case. The label, they argue, tells you a true thing in a way that makes you assume a false one.

### Acto 4 · Los jueces

*Imagen: el edificio del Segundo Circuito. Tarjetas con las frases de los jueces.*

> In October of twenty twenty-five, a federal judge named Jed Rakoff threw the case out. His reasoning included the point the retailers had made themselves: algorithms may lower prices overall, even if not every customer benefits.
>
> The retailers appealed. On the nineteenth of October this year, three judges of the Second Circuit heard the argument.
>
> Their lawyer made a concession in open court that is worth holding onto. He agreed that most consumers would assume the price they are shown is the price everyone pays.
>
> Which is exactly the problem the label exists to fix. Judge Denny Chin said so almost immediately: the notice seems to give consumers useful information in a situation where they would expect everyone to pay the same.
>
> Judge Steven Menashi pushed the other way. If consumers assume a price is universal, can a price by itself be misleading? And is this disclosure actually informing a transaction, or just satisfying consumer curiosity?
>
> The state's lawyer answered that some people would avoid personalised pricing even when it saves them money. That they are entitled to know, and then decide.
>
> No decision yet.

### Acto 5 · Qué significa

*Imagen: la etiqueta digital otra vez, en una tienda vacía al cerrar.*

> Notice what nobody in that courtroom was arguing about.
>
> Nobody was arguing about whether a shop may charge you a different price from the person next to you. That question is settled. They may. The fight is over eleven words on a shelf.
>
> And the strongest argument against those eleven words is that the news might be good. That half the time the machine is cutting the price, and the label makes you flinch anyway.
>
> That argument is probably correct. It is also an argument for less information, made by the people who have all of it.
>
> A price used to be a fact about a product. Now it is increasingly a fact about you — and the open question is no longer what they are allowed to know. It is whether they have to mention it.
>
> Half the price changes went down. That is the number the retailers want in evidence, and they are right that it matters.
>
> They just want it to settle the argument instead of starting it.
