### Acto 2 · La máquina

*Imagen: plano del arco UVeye. Después, Earth Studio sobre un aeropuerto estadounidense.*

> The scanner is made by a company called UVeye. It is an arch, about the size of a car wash entrance. You drive through it slowly and it photographs everything.
>
> Thousands of high resolution images. Underneath the car as well. Before your rental, and after it.
>
> On paper, this is a good idea. Anyone who has argued with a rental desk about a scratch that was already there knows the problem it claims to solve. A human inspector is tired, rushed, and inconsistent. A machine is none of those things.
>
> Hertz installed them at ten airports, with plans to go nationwide.
>
> So far this is a technology story. Here is where it stops being one.

*Imagen: capturas de los cargos automatizados, los campos sensibles tapados.*

> The machine does not just find the damage. It prices it, and it bills you, automatically.
>
> And the bill is not only the repair. There is a processing fee. There is an administrative fee. In Adam Foley's case, the fees were more than twice the damage.
>
> Think about what that means. Eighty dollars of alleged damage generated three hundred and fifty dollars of charges. The dent is not the product. The dent is the trigger.
>
> Hertz says that of more than six hundred and seventy five thousand rentals scanned, over ninety seven percent showed no billable damage.
>
> Hold onto that number, because it sounds reassuring and it is not.

*Acotación: gráfico de Datawrapper. Una barra de 675.000 y, al lado, el 3% destacado.*

> Three percent of six hundred and seventy five thousand rentals is around twenty thousand charges.
>
> Twenty thousand customers, at a few hundred dollars each. That is not a rounding error. That is a revenue line.
>
> And notice what the ninety seven percent figure quietly does. It changes the subject. The question was never how often the machine charges people. The question is whether the charges are right, and what happens to you when they are not.

### Acto 4 · La otra historia

*Imagen: cambio de tono. Del aeropuerto a documentos financieros. Música más baja.*

> While the scanners were rolling out, Hertz was telling investors a story.
>
> On the seventh of May this year, the company announced what it called its strongest revenue growth in three years. It pointed to liquidity of eight hundred and thirty seven million dollars. It talked about improving fleet metrics.
>
> According to the lawsuit that was later filed, Hertz told investors that its liquidity facilities would be sufficient to fund operating activities for the next twelve months.
>
> Sufficient. For twelve months. The seventh of May.

*Acotación: esa frase en pantalla, entrecomillada, con la fecha debajo. Tres segundos quieta.*

> To understand why that matters, you need to know how a rental car company actually makes money. It is not really a service business. It is a balance sheet.
>
> Hertz buys cars. It rents them out for a while. Then it sells them.
>
> The rental income matters, but the number that decides whether the business works is what the car is worth when it is sold. That is depreciation, and it is the largest cost in the business.
>
> So a rental company is, in effect, making a bet on the used car market. If used car prices hold up, the cars cost less than expected and the business prints money. If used car prices fall, every single car in the fleet is worth less than the plan assumed. At the same time.

*Acotación: diagrama animado. Comprar → alquilar → vender, y el valor residual como la variable que decide todo.*

> Now put the scanner next to that.
>
> If a car comes back with a dent, that dent comes off the resale price. Every mark the machine finds and bills is a cost transferred from the company's balance sheet to the customer's credit card.
>
> I want to be careful here, because this is the point where it would be easy to overreach.
>
> I have found no evidence that Hertz installed those scanners in order to cover a financial hole. Nobody has alleged that. The lawsuit does not claim it.
>
> What I can show you is the sequence. And I think the sequence is worth seeing.

### Acto 5 · El veinticuatro de junio

*Imagen: el gráfico de la acción, ese día, cayendo.*

> Six weeks after telling investors its liquidity would last twelve months, Hertz disclosed two things on the same day.
>
> The used car market had softened unexpectedly. And the company had arranged more than three hundred million dollars of distressed financing, involving the lending of over thirty seven million shares.
>
> Distressed financing is what it sounds like. It is the money you raise when the ordinary money is no longer available, and it costs you dearly.
>
> The stock fell more than forty percent in one day and closed at three dollars.

*Acotación: silencio aquí. Dos segundos sin música.*

> In July, shareholders filed a securities fraud class action. The class period runs from the seventh of May to the twenty third of June. Those exact dates.
>
> The complaint alleges that while Hertz was describing its liquidity as sufficient, it was in fact facing rapidly deteriorating liquidity and a likely need for emergency financing that would dilute existing shareholders.
>
> I want to say this clearly. Those are allegations. A court has not ruled on them. Hertz has not been found to have done anything wrong, and companies do sometimes get blindsided by markets without anyone having lied.
>
> The complaint is a public document. You can read it on CourtListener for free and decide what you make of it.

### Acto 6 · Qué significa

*Imagen: vuelta al arco del escáner. Plano largo y quieto.*

> So here is where I land, and you are welcome to land somewhere else.
>
> The way this story was told was: a company installed artificial intelligence, and the artificial intelligence was unfair. That framing is comfortable, because it makes the machine the villain.
>
> But the machine did not decide to add a processing fee. The machine did not set the amount. The machine did not decide that a dispute should be hard. Those are policies. People wrote them.
>
> What the artificial intelligence actually did was make an old thing cheap. Inspecting every returned car closely enough to find every mark used to cost more than the marks were worth. The scanner removed that limit.
>
> And when you remove the cost of enforcement, enforcement expands to fill the space. Not because anyone is evil. Because the business case now works.
>
> That is the thing I think is worth taking away from this, and it is not really about rental cars.
>
> There are a hundred businesses sitting on rules they have never fully enforced, because enforcing them cost more than it returned. Late fees. Usage limits. Wear and tear. Terms in a contract nobody reads.
>
> Artificial intelligence does not change those rules. It just makes them enforceable for the first time.
>
> Hertz happened to be first, and it happened to be first while its own numbers were turning against it. I cannot tell you those two facts are connected.
>
> I can tell you they happened in that order.
